There is no universal number. The right volume of creative depends on how much you spend, because every test needs enough budget to produce a result you can trust. What can be said precisely is how to work out your number and how to make each test count.

## Why does budget set the limit?

Each creative needs enough delivery to show whether it beats your current best ad. If you split a small budget across many ads, none gets enough spend to judge, and the platform will push most spend to one or two ads anyway.

A useful way to size it:

1. Estimate the spend needed for one ad to produce a meaningful number of conversions at your typical cost per result.
2. Decide what share of total budget goes to testing; many advertisers use a minority share and keep most spend on proven ads.
3. Divide the testing budget by the spend per test. That is roughly how many concepts you can test properly in the period.

## What counts as a new creative?

Not every change is equal:

| Level | Example | What you learn |
|---|---|---|
| Concept | A new idea or angle: price, problem, proof, objection | Which message resonates |
| Format | Video vs static vs carousel | Which format the audience responds to |
| Hook | The first seconds or the headline | What stops the scroll |
| Variation | Colour, button, minor copy changes | Usually little |

Test concepts first. A new concept with a weak hook can still teach you something; ten colour variations of the same ad rarely do.

## How should a test be structured?

- **One variable at a time** when you want to learn why something worked.
- **Against the current winner**, not in isolation.
- **With a stopping rule decided in advance**, based on spend or conversions, not on how the first day looks.
- **Judged on the business metric**, such as cost per qualified lead or purchase, with hook rate and hold rate as diagnostics.

## What is creative fatigue?

As the same people see an ad repeatedly, response declines and costs rise. Rising frequency with falling click-through or conversion rates is the usual sign. Larger accounts reach fatigue faster because they reach their audience more often, which is why they need more creative volume.

## Where should new ideas come from?

- Customer reviews and support questions: the words customers use
- Sales calls: the objections that come up most
- Your best-performing past ads: new angles on the same message
- Competitors' long-running ads, which usually signal what works in the category

## The practical answer

Start with what your budget supports, test distinct concepts against your best ad, and increase volume as spend grows or fatigue appears.
